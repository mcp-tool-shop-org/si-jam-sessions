# Lane 4 — real-time AI co-performance (Opus research seat, 2026-09-25; retrieval claimed, NOT yet gated)

Not retrieved: Bencina "Real-time audio programming 101" (connection refused; archive.org blocked); Rottondi et al. 2016 body (abstract only); full PDFs of Chafe 2010 and Raphael 2010; Rasch 1979 (only via Deutsch).

1. Delay drags tempo. Chafe, Cáceres & Gurevich 2010, "Effect of temporal separation on synchronization in rhythmic performance", Perception 39(7), doi:10.1068/p6465 — clapping pairs with 3–78 ms one-way delay: shortest delays accelerate, longer ones progressively decelerate until the rhythm falls apart [abs]; CCRMA page (https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~cc/shtml/ensDelay.shtml) puts the acceleration zone below 11.5 ms. → keep engine-to-human heard delay near 10 ms.
2. What "together" means. Deutsch 2010, "Hearing music in ensembles", Physics Today 63(2), doi:10.1063/1.3326988, reporting Rasch 1979 — nominally simultaneous trio notes 30–50 ms apart; 10 ms already sounds more separate, 30 ms clearly separate [body]. → score "together" with ±30–50 ms tolerance, render at sample precision.
3. The audio tap cannot gate. Hu, Peter, Schlüter & Widmer 2025, ISMIR, arXiv:2509.07586 — prior live transcribers lag 128–320 ms; interactive use needs < 30 ms; strict causality costs accuracy [abs]; shifted window cuts preprocessing 64 → 10 ms but that window + causal convs cause the largest accuracy losses [body]. → tap verifies committed intents after a lag; never blocks a quantum.
4. Predict, don't react. Raphael 2010, "Music Plus One and Machine Learning", ICML 21–28, https://mlanthology.org/icml/2010/raphael2010icml-music/ — Kalman-like predictor of future timing links follower to renderer [abs]. Cont 2010, Antescofo, IEEE TPAMI 32(6), doi:10.1109/TPAMI.2009.106 — audio and tempo agents feed each other predictions [abs]. → a deterministic tempo predictor lives inside the law.
5. Commit horizons absorb latency. Scarlatos et al. 2025, ReaLJam, CHI EA, arXiv:2502.21267 (on ReaLchords, ICML 2024, PMLR 235:53328–53345) — 1/16-note steps, agent plans 4 beats ahead, chords inside the commit window can no longer change; slow responses harmless while planned frames exceed waiting frames; most responses < 100 ms; musicians split on committing 0 vs 4 beats ahead [body]. → accept proposals only beyond the commit horizon; a committed quantum never changes.
6. The budget is calculable. Zheng et al. 2026, StreamMUSE, RTAS, arXiv:2606.11886 — tick = 1/16 note (125 ms at 120 BPM), client owns the clock; works iff RT95 ≤ I and GL ≥ I + RT95 (RT95 = p95 round trip, I = request interval, GL = generated length, all in ticks); base latency ~100 ms LAN / ~150 ms cloud; >60% of cloud configurations missed real time; faster tempos shrink the feasible set [body]. → size the model's planning horizon from measured p95 latency.
7. Generators plan seconds ahead. Lyria Team 2025, Live Music Models, arXiv:2508.04651 — Magenta RealTime: 2 s chunks from 10 s context, 1.8× real time on H100; style prompts "may take two or more seconds" [body]. Thickstun et al. 2023 (TMLR 2024), arXiv:2306.08620 — each control placed 5 s ahead of the events it shapes [body]. Wu et al. 2025, arXiv:2510.22105 — coherence improves sharply with a little future input [body]. → the LLM steers phrases; note-level anticipation must be trained and tested.
8. Slow planner, fast clock. SuperCollider "Scheduling and Server timing", https://doc.sccode.org/Guides/ServerTiming.html — un-timestamped messages drift ~2 ms per event; bundles timestamped with 0.2 s default latency. Wilson 2013, "A Tale of Two Clocks", https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling — JS timers off by tens of ms; a 25 ms timer schedules 100 ms ahead on the audio clock. → every intent carries an audio-clock timestamp; nothing is "play now".
9. Latency as musical form. Cockos NINJAM, https://www.cockos.com/ninjam/ — latency "measured in measures"; each player plays with the others' previous interval. Ableton Link docs, https://ableton.github.io/link/ — shared quantum in beats keeps beat positions aligned. Kim et al. 2026, "A Design Space for Live Music Agents", CHI, arXiv:2602.05064 — of 184 systems, 5% take turns, 3% lead; latency the top technical concern (85%) [body]. → one beat timeline for all engines; call-and-response one phrase later is an under-used slot.
Rollback: no study applies game rollback to music; nearest is TablaNet (Sarkar, MIT S.M. 2007, https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/41750) — plays a predicted remote stroke before the real one [abs]. Seat's inference: sound already played cannot be rolled back, so only uncommitted plans can be speculative.
10. Render thread. PortAudio "Writing a Callback Function", https://files.portaudio.com/docs/v19-doxydocs/writing_a_callback.html — no allocation, I/O, context switches or mutexes; Windows/macOS schedulers lack real-time-safe priority-inversion prevention. Microsoft Learn "Low Latency Audio", https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/audio/low-latency-audio — default buffer 10 ms; HDAudio 128–480 samples (2.66–10 ms at 48 kHz); engine adds 1.3 ms. → the callback only copies committed frames from a lock-free queue.

Recommendation (seat): quantum = one 128-frame device buffer at 48 kHz (2.67 ms), hashed each quantum; intents carry sample offset + 1/16 position; law commits one quantum ahead; a routed human note sounds after ~3 quanta (~8 ms, seat's arithmetic) — under the 10 ms target, 30 ms hard ceiling. Model sits off the audio clock: proposes phrase plans only for uncommitted bars ≥ p95 latency + commit horizon ahead (≈ 2 bars at 120 BPM for a ~3 s LLM); a deterministic tempo follower places notes; late plans dropped. Host MAY set buffer size, compensate output latency, timestamp human input on the audio clock, render committed frames; MAY NOT choose musical content or timing, admit or reorder intents, block/allocate in the callback, or let the tap block a quantum.
